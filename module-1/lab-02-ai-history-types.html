2 · The Big Picture

AI history is a story of changing ideas about intelligence

The supplied resources describe AI as developing through several overlapping lines of work: mathematical models inspired by neurons, questions about whether machines can think, symbolic reasoning and problem solving, machine learning, natural language processing, robotics and increasingly data-driven/deep-learning systems.

ARTIFICIAL
INTELLIGENCE

🧠 Thinking & Reasoning

Neural models, logic, theorem proving, problem solving, knowledge and reasoning.

📊 Learning from Data

Machine learning, reinforcement learning, deep learning and pattern recognition.

Key lesson: AI did not suddenly appear with modern generative systems. The course history begins with foundational work in the 1940s and 1950s and follows several decades of changing approaches.
3 · History Timeline

Major milestones in the course resources

The timeline below follows the supplied Module 1 handout and Reema Thareja's Chapter 1. Russell & Norvig is used as a complementary historical reference for the early foundations.

1943 — Artificial neuron model

Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed a mathematical model of artificial neurons. Russell & Norvig describes this as the first work now generally recognized as AI. The model connected ideas from neuron physiology, propositional logic and computation.

1949 — Hebbian learning

Donald Hebb described a rule for modifying connection strengths between neurons. This became known as Hebbian learning and influenced later thinking about learning in neural networks.

1950 — Turing Test & early neural computing

Alan Turing's work on Computing Machinery and Intelligence raised the question “Can machines think?” and introduced the Turing Test. The same year, Marvin Minsky and Dean Edmonds built SNARC, an early neural-network computer; Claude Shannon published work on computer chess.

1952 — Self-learning checkers

Arthur Samuel developed a self-learning program for checkers. This became an important early demonstration that a program could improve its play through experience.

1954 — Machine translation experiment

The course text records an IBM computer translating 60 carefully selected Russian sentences into English, illustrating early interest in language processing.

1956 — Dartmouth Conference

John McCarthy coined the term Artificial Intelligence in connection with the Dartmouth workshop. The course treats this as the birth of AI as a formal field. Allen Newell and Herbert Simon also demonstrated the Logic Theorist, an early reasoning program.

1958 — Lisp

John McCarthy developed Lisp, an AI programming language that became highly influential. He also worked on ideas for AI systems based on knowledge and reasoning.

1959 — GPS & “machine learning”

Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and J. C. Shaw developed the General Problem Solver (GPS), intended to imitate human problem solving. Arthur Samuel coined the term machine learning.

1963–1969 — AI labs, ELIZA & expert systems

John McCarthy started the Stanford AI Lab in 1963. Joseph Weizenbaum developed ELIZA, an early natural-language program. By 1969, an expert system for diagnosing blood infections had been developed at Stanford.

1972 — PROLOG

The logic programming language PROLOG was created, becoming important for symbolic AI and logic programming.

1974–1980 — First AI Winter

The course text identifies this period as the First AI Winter, when funding and expectations fell and AI research slowed.

1980 — Commercial expert systems

DEC developed R1, described by the course text as the first successful commercial expert system.

1982–1983 — Fifth Generation project

Japan launched the ambitious Fifth Generation Computer Systems project. The US later increased support for advanced computing and AI research.

1987–1993 — Second AI Winter

The course describes another period of reduced expectations and funding. Japan terminated the Fifth Generation project in 1992; the US ended related efforts in 1993.

1997 — Deep Blue defeats Kasparov

IBM's Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov. In the course framework, Deep Blue is an example of a reactive machine.

2005 — STANLEY & autonomous systems

STANLEY, a self-driving car, won the DARPA Grand Challenge. The course also notes increasing military investment in autonomous robots.

2008 — Speech recognition

Google made a notable advance in speech recognition and introduced the feature in its iPhone application.

2011 — Siri

Apple released Siri, an AI-powered virtual assistant. In the course framework, Siri is a classic example of Weak/Narrow AI.

2012 — Deep learning breakthrough

Andrew Ng's Google Brain work used 10 million YouTube videos as training data for a neural network that learned to recognize cats without being explicitly told what a cat was.

2014 — Self-driving car & Alexa

The course text records Google's self-driving car passing a state driving test and Amazon releasing Alexa.

2016 — AlphaGo & Sophia

Google DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated Go world champion Lee Sedol, a major AI milestone. The same year, the course records the creation of Sophia, a humanoid robot capable of facial recognition, verbal communication and facial expression.

2018 — BERT & Waymo One

Google released BERT, an NLP engine that improved language understanding/translation-related tasks. Waymo One was launched as a self-driving vehicle service.

2020 — LinearFold

Baidu released LinearFold, which the course text describes as predicting viral RNA sequences rapidly during the early SARS-CoV-2 pandemic.

5 · Two Ways to Classify AI

Capability vs Functionality

🎯 Based on capability

Weak AI → designed for a specific task.

Strong AI → aims to resemble broad human intelligence and perform tasks beyond specific training.

⚙️ Based on functionality

Reactive → reacts to current input.

Limited Memory → uses relevant past/short-term information.

Theory of Mind → would model thoughts/emotions.

Self-Awareness → would possess consciousness of itself.

Exam alert: “Weak vs Strong” and “Reactive vs Limited Memory vs Theory of Mind vs Self-Awareness” are different classification dimensions. Do not mix them as if they are the same four categories.
6 · Weak AI

Weak AI / Narrow AI

PURPOSE

Specific task

Weak AI is specifically designed to perform a particular type of task. It operates within a limited context.

EXAMPLES

Siri · Alexa · Search

The textbook gives Siri and Alexa as examples and also lists Google Search, image recognition software, self-driving cars and IBM Watson as examples of narrow AI applications.

WHY IMPORTANT?

Most practical AI today

The course describes Weak AI as the most successful realization of AI to date and notes its societal benefits.

Remember: “Weak” does not mean “useless.” It means the intelligence is narrowly scoped to a task or set of related tasks.
7 · Strong AI

Strong AI / AGI / Superintelligence in the course text

The supplied material describes Strong AI as an attempt to resemble human thinking. It would require capabilities such as visual perception, speech recognition, decision-making and language translation and would be able to perform tasks for which it was not specifically trained.

🧠 General capability

Unlike a narrowly scoped system, the goal is broad cognitive capability.

🔄 Transfer to new tasks

The course associates Strong AI with performing tasks beyond those specifically trained for.

🎬 Current status in resource

The material says general AI is seen mainly in science fiction and is not yet a reality.

9 · Reactive Machines

Functionality Type 1 — React to the present

👁️

🎯

⚡

🚫
No memory

Reactive machines are the simplest functionality category in the course. They have no memory to store past experiences and respond to the current situation. The supplied textbook uses IBM Deep Blue as an example: it evaluates the present chess-board state and chooses a move without referring to previous games.

Key property: Current percept → response. No stored past experience is used to improve future decisions.
10 · Limited Memory

Functionality Type 2 — Use relevant past information

Limited Memory machines retain data for a limited period and use it for decisions. The course particularly connects them with autonomous vehicles, which may use recent information such as the speed of nearby cars, distances and speed limits.

Three ML models named in the resource

Reinforcement Learning

Learns through repeated trial and error to make better predictions/decisions.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

Uses past data to help predict the next item in a sequence, giving more priority to recent information while still considering past data.

Evolutionary GANs (E-GAN)

The text describes a model that evolves over time using previous experience, simulations and statistics to explore better decisions.

11 · Theory of Mind

Functionality Type 3 — Understanding thoughts and emotions

💭
Thoughts

Would represent aspects of another agent's internal mental state.

❤️
Emotions

Would account for feelings that affect behaviour.

🧠
Memories

The course describes representations beginning from thoughts, emotions and memories.

Status: The supplied textbook explicitly describes Theory of Mind systems as theoretical and says such systems do not currently exist as described.
12 · Self-Awareness

Functionality Type 4 — Awareness of its own state

🪞
Self-state

The system would understand its own state and existence in the world.

🤝
Others' feelings

The resource describes using self-awareness to deduce or interpret others' feelings.

🧩
Conscious context

Knowledge and conscious context would influence responses to events.

Status: The course material says self-aware AI systems do not yet exist but may become available in the future.

16 · Common Misconceptions
“Weak AI means a bad AI.”

No. In the course, “weak/narrow” describes scope. A narrow system can be extremely effective at its intended task.

“Strong AI and Limited Memory are the same classification.”

No. Weak/Strong classify AI by capability; Reactive/Limited Memory/Theory of Mind/Self-Awareness classify it by functionality.

“Deep Blue remembers every previous chess game.”

The supplied course framework uses Deep Blue as a reactive machine and emphasizes that reactive machines do not use stored past experiences.

“Theory of Mind AI already understands human emotions exactly like humans.”

The supplied textbook presents Theory of Mind as theoretical and says such systems do not currently exist as described.

“Self-aware AI is the same as a chatbot that says ‘I am conscious.’”

A statement generated by a system is not evidence of actual self-awareness. The course definition requires awareness of its own existence/state and consciousness.

19 · Takeaways
  • 1943: McCulloch and Pitts' artificial-neuron model is a foundational milestone.
  • 1950: Turing's work introduced the Turing Test and strengthened the question of machine intelligence.
  • 1956: Dartmouth is the landmark associated with the formal birth of AI as a field in the course material.
  • Weak AI: narrow/specific-task intelligence and widely used today.
  • Strong AI: aims at broad, human-like/general intelligence and is presented as future/theoretical in the supplied text.
  • Reactive: current input only; no past experience.
  • Limited Memory: uses limited/relevant past information.
  • Theory of Mind: would model thoughts/emotions; theoretical.
  • Self-Awareness: would possess awareness of its own state/existence; not yet established.
